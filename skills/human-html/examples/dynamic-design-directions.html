Which trigger fires a relay digest

relay, the internal notifications service, still sends one message per platform event. Four candidate batching shapes were built far enough to replay, and all four were replayed against the same recorded day of traffic. The shape of this artifact follows from that: the input is pinned first, then each direction is judged against it, then the pick.

3direction picked

Give each recipient a fixed slot inside every 15 minute period, and keep direction 4's 20 minute age cap underneath it as a guard. On the replayed day it is the only direction whose peak second stays at 11 sends without touching the vendor cap of 120, and the only one whose outage cost holds up wherever the outage falls: a 3m 20s run of vendor 502s costs it 470 digests against direction 1's 4,180. Direction 4 loses fewer, 190, but only because this particular outage missed its burst by two minutes. It does not ship until relay_pending gets a composite index, which is blocked on the current migration freeze.

In plain terms

  • What changes for someone receiving notifications: instead of a message per event, one digest arrives holding everything from the last few minutes, and nothing in it has been waiting more than 30 minutes.
  • Why this needs a decision and not a preference: the four shapes sit within 26% of each other on digests sent per day, and they span 190 to 4,180 on how many digests are lost when the mail vendor has a bad three minutes. The second number is the one that generates support tickets.
  • What is being asked: approve the per-recipient slot trigger plus the 20 minute age cap, and grant one exception to the relay_pending migration freeze so the index can land.

Everything below is measured against a single recorded day, described in the next section, so no direction is being scored on a quieter example than the others.

Control: one input, replayed four times

The data all four are judged on

One file: relay/traces/2026-07-14-mon.jsonl, replayed through relay/bench/replay.py --direction N with the mail vendor stubbed by the recorded response log. Monday is the heaviest weekday, so this is a pessimistic input, and it is pessimistic for all four in the same way. Every number in the four panels below comes from this file. None of them comes from a hand-picked hour.

Four properties of that file matter more than its size, and they are the four that pull the directions apart:

  • Latency is a constraint, not a target. A digest must reach the recipient within 30 minutes of the oldest event it contains. That sentence is already printed on the notification settings page, so a direction that misses it is not a slower option, it is a wrong one.
  • The vendor caps sends at 120 per second per account. Above that the mailgrid send API returns 429 and the client queues locally, which turns a burst into latency rather than into errors.
  • Failures arrive in a clump, not spread out. Every call between 09:14:40 and 09:18:00 returned HTTP 502; scattered singles elsewhere bring the day's total to 0.4% of calls. Where that clump falls relative to a direction's flush pattern decides what it costs, which is why the window's placement is stated here rather than inside a panel.
  • The recipient distribution has no middle. 6,701 recipients (71%) receive between 1 and 3 events across the whole day. 38 receive more than 200. The import at 08:58 touches 4,180 of them, at an average of 1.6 events each.

That last property is why no single tuned constant settles this: a threshold that batches sensibly for the 71% either starves or floods the 38, and both groups are visible in the same replay.

One place this trace flatters a direction

The 502 window starts at 09:14:40, under three minutes after the import stops writing. Direction 4 flushes on a 20 minute age cap, so the import's own digests land from 09:20 onwards, after the window has closed, and all the window catches is the thin pre-import tail still flushing through it. It scores well on outage cost by accident of where the recording falls. That is called out in its own panel rather than quietly banked.

Current vs proposed delivery shape

All four directions make the same two structural changes: an event becomes a row in relay_pending instead of a send, and a separate flusher turns pending rows into one rendered digest. Nothing else about the pipeline moves. The four differ in one box, and drawing that box is most of what keeps the comparison honest.

Because the shared part is identical, the shared costs are identical too: one new table, one flusher process, one render path that takes a list of events rather than one. Those are not weighed again per direction.

Direction 1

Sweep a fixed 15 minute window

A cron runs at :00, :15, :30 and :45. Each run selects every unsent row, groups by recipient, renders, sends. 96 runs a day, no per-recipient state, nothing to hold in your head between runs.

That is the finding worth carrying forward. The input never contained a one-second burst; the fixed boundary manufactures one.

Everything else about direction 1 is comfortable. Worst-case latency is 15m 35s, inside the 30 minute commitment with 14 minutes spare. The busiest recipient gets 41 digests across the day, one per period in which they were active. Cost is a single number, 96 runs, and it does not move with volume.

The failure interaction is where the comfort stops. The 09:15 sweep runs from 09:15:00 to 09:15:35, entirely inside the recorded 502 window, so all 4,180 sends fail together. The sweep keeps no attempt record, so those rows stay unsent until the 09:30 sweep picks them up alongside everything new. All 4,180 recipients then get one digest at 09:30 carrying 30 minutes of events instead of 15, and neither the digest nor the flusher log says a first attempt was ever made.

Take
The flat cost model. 96 runs a day is one number to bill, one thing to alert on, and one place to look when digests stop arriving.
Leave
The sweep-wide select. A per-recipient claim row with its own attempt counter is what turns 4,180 all-or-nothing sends into 4,180 independently retryable ones, and it is needed under every direction here.

Both halves of that verdict survive into the pick: the cost model as a reason the fixed period stays, the claim row as work that happens whichever trigger wins.

Direction 2

Wait for a quiet gap

Each event sets a 5 minute timer for its recipient. A new event resets the timer. The digest goes out after 5 quiet minutes, so a burst of related activity arrives as one message once the activity stops.

For the 71% who get 1 to 3 events a day this reads better than anything else on offer. Two events at 09:04 and 09:06 become one digest at 09:11, and the digest holds exactly the things that belong together.

For the top 38 it fails the constraint outright. The busiest recipient receives an event more often than every 5 minutes from 09:00 until 12:42, so the timer never expires. The first digest lands at 12:47 holding 431 events: the newest is 5 minutes old, the oldest is 3h 47m old, and the 30 minute commitment is missed by more than three hours. A hard ceiling on top fixes that, and a quiet gap plus a hard ceiling is direction 4 with an extra timer.

A second problem hides inside a single-process replay. The flusher runs three replicas in production and the timers live in each process, so a recipient whose rows are visible to two replicas gets two digests. Moving the timers into Redis is possible; it also means 9,412 live keys with a reset on every event, which is a busier write path than either of the period-based directions needs.

Take
The quiet-gap reading for chat delivery specifically, where 5 idle minutes is a real conversational pause and a digest genuinely wants to match it. That is a follow-up for the chat transport, not a reason to run the mail path this way.
Leave
The quiet gap as the only trigger. Starvation here is not a rare shape, it is the 38 heaviest recipients on every weekday morning.

Direction 2 is out on the constraint, not on cost. That is the cleanest kind of elimination available, so its other numbers are not argued over further.

Direction 3

Give each recipient a slot

Each recipient gets a fixed offset from a stable hash of their id, crc32(recipient_id) % 900, and their digest goes out at that second of every 15 minute period. The flusher wakes once a second and asks for the rows whose slot is now.

The property being bought is that the send rate does not depend on the arrival shape at all. Directions 2 and 4 also stayed under the cap on this replay, but only because the import writes over 14 minutes and they inherit that spread. Direction 3 would stay at 11 sends per second if the import wrote all 6,830 events in one statement, because slot assignment never looks at when an event arrived.

That matters now rather than in principle. relay/ingest/bulk.py lands this quarter and replaces the per-record import loop with a single insert. Replayed against that path, the peak second for directions 1, 2 and 4 becomes 120, 120 and 120. For direction 3 it stays at 11.

The failure interaction is the mildest of the four. The 502 window covers 180 of the 900 slots in the 09:15 period plus 20 slots at the tail of the 09:00 period, so 470 digests fail instead of 4,180, and each retries in its own next slot 15 minutes later without colliding with anyone else's retry. Worst-case latency is 14m 59s, for a recipient whose event lands one second after their slot has passed.

The cost is the wake-up rate. One tick a second is 86,400 queries a day where direction 1 does 96. The queries are trivial, but only with a composite index on (flush_slot, sent_at): without it the replay showed a 340ms sequential scan per tick against a one-second budget, which is a slow saturation rather than a clean failure. Bucketing slots into 5 second groups cuts the tick rate to 17,280 a day and costs 4 seconds of latency, and no measurement here argues against doing that.

One caveat, stated plainly because it is easy to forget later: recipient ids are dense sequential integers today, and crc32 % 900 spreads them close to uniformly, with the 38 heaviest recipients landing in 31 distinct slots so no slot carries two heavy digests. That is a property of the current id space, not of the hash.

Take
The stable per-recipient offset, bucketed to 5 seconds, and the per-slot retry that falls out of it for nothing.
Leave
The one-second tick as written, and the assumption that the hash keeps spreading well after the move to opaque recipient ids. Both are re-measurable rather than structural.

Neither half of that verdict names a defect in the shape itself. The other three each have one.

Direction 4

Count ceiling with an age cap

Flush when a recipient has 20 pending events, or when their oldest pending event turns 20 minutes old, whichever comes first. Two triggers, both checked on the same tick.

The age cap is the useful half. It gives the tightest worst-case latency of the four, 20m 02s, and it does so without knowing anything about volume. 74% of the day's digests fire on it, so for most recipients the count ceiling is decoration.

The count ceiling is the problem half, and it is a tuning trap rather than a bug. At 20 events the busiest recipient receives 58 digests across the day, which is not a digest cadence, it is the current per-event behaviour at a 20× discount. Raise the ceiling to 60 and it fires 412 times a day across all recipients instead of 3,170, at which point the age cap does all the work and the ceiling is a second code path with no measurable job. No setting is right for both the 71% and the 38.

The concrete failure is the retry. When a send fails the pending rows stay pending, and their oldest event is by then older than 20 minutes, so the age cap is satisfied again on the very next tick. The replay recorded 9 sends for one digest in the 61 seconds from 09:16:59, all nine answered with a 502, and what stopped the loop was the vendor recovering at 09:18:00 rather than anything in the flusher. Fixing it needs an attempt counter and a backoff column, which is the same claim-row work direction 1 needs, so it is not an argument against direction 4 specifically. The scoring below assumes that fix is in place for all four.

On outage cost direction 4 loses 190 digests, the best of the four, and that number should not be trusted. Its flush distribution is a 20 minute delayed copy of the arrival distribution, so its exposure depends entirely on where an outage falls. The window happens to cover 09:14:40 to 09:18:00, which for direction 4 is the sparse tail of pre-import traffic; the import's own 4,180 digests flush from 09:20, two minutes after the vendor came back. An outage twenty minutes later would have hit it as hard as the 09:15 sweep hits direction 1.

Take
The 20 minute age cap, as a floor under whatever else fires. It is the cheapest way to make the 30 minute commitment structural rather than emergent.
Leave
The count ceiling as a trigger. Tuned high it never fires; tuned low it reproduces per-event delivery for the people who most need batching.

Direction 4 contributes a guard, not a shape. It appears in the pick on exactly those terms.

Read the last row against the first: directions 2 and 4 stay under the vendor cap only while arrivals are spread across 14 minutes, so their good peak number belongs to the input rather than to the design. Direction 3 stays under it either way, and the input changes this quarter.

The call. Adopt direction 3's assigned per-recipient slot as the flush trigger, bucketed into 5 second groups, with direction 4's 20 minute age cap as a guard for any recipient whose slot has been skipped. This is not a fifth direction: it is direction 3 plus one guard clause, and the guard exists so a missed tick or a paused flusher cannot quietly push a digest past the published 30 minute commitment.

What it costs, in order of who has to act. A composite index on relay_pending (flush_slot, sent_at): that one needs an exception to the migration freeze, and without it the tick degrades into a sequential scan rather than failing loudly, the worse of the two ways to be wrong. A per-recipient claim row with an attempt counter and a backoff column: directions 1 and 4 need it too, and nobody has scheduled it. A slot column backfilled for 9,412 recipients, cheap and reversible. And a re-measurement of the hash spread whenever recipient ids stop being dense integers.

None of that is large. The index exception is the only item with a dependency outside relay, and it is the only reason this cannot be implemented the week it is approved.

What would change the pick

Four things would reopen this. Each is listed with what it would move to, rather than as a risk in the abstract:

  1. The bulk import path gets dropped or deferred past this quarter. The strongest argument for direction 3 is that its send rate ignores arrival shape, and that only pays once arrivals can spike. If relay/ingest/bulk.py is shelved, direction 1 becomes the cheaper answer: 96 cron runs beat a per-second tick and an index exception.
  2. The vendor cap rises above the peak. The higher mailgrid tier quotes 1,000 sends per second. At that ceiling the 09:15 sweep drains in 4.2 seconds and most of the flat-load argument evaporates, again leaving direction 1 in front.
  3. The latency commitment tightens from 30 minutes to 10. That has been floated for chat delivery. A 10 minute commitment forces the period down to about 5 minutes and the slot space down to 300 seconds, and at 300 slots the heaviest recipients start sharing slots, which is the load-spreading property this pick was chosen for. That is a re-comparison, not a constant to retune.
  4. The migration freeze holds through the quarter. Then direction 3 is not shippable, and the fallback is direction 1 with the 20 minute age cap bolted on, accepting the 4,180 digest exposure during a vendor outage until the index lands.

Three of those four point back at direction 1. The fixed sweep is the runner-up and the fallback, not a discarded option.

The honest limit on all of this is that it is one Monday. The trace is the heaviest weekday, and a quieter day would flatter every direction in roughly the same proportion, so the ordering is unlikely to move. But nothing here has been replayed against a month, the 502 window is a single recorded outage rather than a distribution, and the chat transport was not modelled at all.
